This report translates the cross-validated output from your three-layer model stack into a business-readable narrative. The main idea is simple: lower RMSE means better magnitude forecasting, higher DA means better direction calls, and the two do not always move together.

Executive Read

Layer 2a is the weakest model almost everywhere. Splitting the sample only by high/low volatility does not seem rich enough for these markets.

Layer 2b is the most stable upgrade. It rarely wins by a lot, but it usually matches or slightly improves on Layer 1 RMSE without introducing much extra instability.

Layer 3 is the most interesting but also the most uneven. It produces the best RMSE in several short-horizon settings, especially for TTF h=1, TTF h=3, POWER h=1, POWER h=5, and EUA h=1, but it sometimes gives up direction accuracy or deteriorates at longer horizons.

What Stands Out

1. TTF prefers richer structure only at short horizons

For TTF, Layer 3 wins on RMSE at h=1 and h=3, which suggests regime structure matters for near-term gas forecasting. But by h=5, the regime model loses its edge and Layer 2b becomes the best magnitude forecaster.

Translation: regimes help in the short run, but the long run may be too noisy or too mean-reverting for the Markov layer to hold up.

2. POWER is where Layer 3 looks most promising

Layer 3 is best on both RMSE and DA at POWER h=1 and POWER h=5. That is a strong sign that electricity returns are reacting to latent operating regimes that a linear baseline only partially captures.

POWER h=3 is the exception, where Layer 1 or 2b still looks safer.

3. EUA shows a classic magnitude-vs-direction tradeoff

For EUA, Layer 3 is excellent at h=1, but at h=3 and h=5 it tends to improve direction accuracy more than RMSE. That means it may be useful for directional positioning even when it is not the best continuous-value forecaster.

If the objective is trading sign, Layer 3 becomes more attractive than if the objective is point-forecast precision.

Quick Recommendation by Use Case

If you care most about forecast magnitude

  • TTF: use L3 for h=1 and h=3, then move to L2b at h=5.
  • POWER: use L3 for h=1 and h=5, but L2b or L1 for h=3.
  • EUA: use L3 for h=1, then L1 or L2b for longer horizons.

If you care most about getting direction right

  • TTF: stay with L1 or L2b. Layer 3 lowers RMSE but hurts sign accuracy at h=1.
  • POWER: L3 is especially compelling at h=1 and h=5.
  • EUA: L3 is the best directional model in all three horizons, even when it is not always the best RMSE model.

What the Regime Model Is Telling You

TTF Regimes

Normal state is almost absent. Your sample spends only 0.6% of the time there, while dual-stress and gas-crisis states dominate.

Gas crisis is persistent. Once in crisis, the model stays there with about 87.5% probability next period.

Interpretation: for 2021-2024, the gas market is not behaving like a calm market with rare disruptions. Stress is the base case.

POWER Regimes

Two broad states dominate. Normal and dual-stress each account for roughly half the sample, while the most extreme state is rare.

Interpretation: the power market seems to switch between ordinary and elevated-stress conditions more than it lives in full crisis.

Why this matters: it helps explain why Layer 3 works especially well for POWER at some horizons.

EUA Regimes

Crisis is rare but meaningful. The crisis state appears only 3.5% of the time, but it carries a much higher variance.

Interpretation: carbon behaves like a market that is usually in normal or moderately stressed conditions, with occasional high-variance bursts.

Why this matters: that pattern is consistent with Layer 3 improving directional calls without always improving point forecast RMSE.

The single most important caveat in these results is that “better RMSE” and “better trading usefulness” are not the same thing.

Several Layer 3 runs improve RMSE while hurting direction accuracy, and some EUA results do the opposite. That means your final model choice should be tied to the decision problem: price forecasting, risk estimation, or directional trading signal generation.
